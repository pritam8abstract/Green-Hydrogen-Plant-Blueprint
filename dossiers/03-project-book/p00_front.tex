% ============================ VISION ==================================
\phase{Vision}{A plant a banker would fund}{What we decided, why we decided it, and the one sentence this whole book is trying to prove or disprove.}

Dossier 1 asked which machine. Dossier 2 asked where the money is. This book asks the only question a project manager is really paid to answer: \textbf{can this thing be built on time, run reliably and paid for, and what has to be true for all three at once?}

We have made our choice. Our plant is a \textbf{100 MW hybrid}: 80 MW of pressurised alkaline electrolysers as the workhorse, and 20 MW of PEM electrolysers as the fast-moving part that follows the swings of solar and wind. The PEM slot is also our \textbf{option bay}. When its stacks wear out, around year eleven, we can drop in anion-exchange membrane (AEM) stacks instead, if AEM has proven itself by then. We sell hydrogen, not ammonia, to a refinery next door on a 25-year fixed price, because that is the one Indian customer who has already signed contracts of that kind \cite{jmkiocl2025,rwnrl2026}. Ammonia stays as a phase-two option.

Three beliefs carry over from the first two dossiers and become rules for this one.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.6em,label={\color{audit}\display\arabic*}]
\item \textbf{A project is a promise with a price tag and a date.} We judge every choice by what it does to cost, schedule and the probability of keeping both. A single-point plan is a guess wearing a suit; we plan in ranges and report P50 and P80.
\item \textbf{The lender is our toughest stakeholder.} Seventy per cent of the money is debt. If the cash flow cannot cover the debt by a margin of 1.3 times in a bad year, nothing else in this book matters.
\item \textbf{Keep the future cheap.} The core of the plant uses proven technology. The new technology (AEM) and the second phase are bought as \emph{options}: small payments today for the right, not the duty, to act later.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{plainwords}[Four numbers to keep in your head]
\textbf{₹522 per kg} is what our plant would need to charge, as first designed, to keep its lender and its owners whole. \textbf{₹397 per kg} is what the best Indian refinery contract actually pays \cite{jmkiocl2025}. \textbf{₹389 per kg} is what the plant needs after five design-to-cost moves, so it squeaks through, with ₹8 of headroom. And \textbf{57\%} is the chance that, even then, the plant breaks its loan covenant at least once in fifteen years. Those four numbers are the story of this book.
\end{plainwords}
